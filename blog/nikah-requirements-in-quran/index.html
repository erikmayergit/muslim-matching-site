Nikah Requirements in the Quran: What Makes a Marriage Valid

﷽

In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Bestower of Mercy.

We try to follow and explain from the Qur'an and the authentic Hadith, upon the understanding of the three rightly-guided generations (the Salaf as-Salih). All good is from Allah alone, and any mistake or shortcoming is from ourselves.

TL;DR: The Quran frames nikah as a solemn covenant (mithaqan ghaliza) and, read with the authentic Sunnah, a valid marriage rests on a few clear pillars: two eligible willing Muslim spouses, the woman's wali, the mahr, and public witnessing, with both parties free of any lawful impediment. Miss one of these and the contract is broken, not merely imperfect.

Marriage in Islam is not a private arrangement between two people who feel ready. It is a contract with conditions set by Allah, and the Quran describes it in weighty terms. In Surah an-Nisa, Allah calls the marriage bond a "solemn covenant" (Qur'an 4:21). In Surah ar-Rum He describes its purpose: "And among His signs is that He created for you mates from among yourselves that you may find tranquillity in them, and He placed between you love and mercy" (Qur'an 30:21). A covenant of that weight has requirements, and taking them lightly is how people end up in unions that were never valid to begin with.

Below are the requirements for a sound nikah, grounded in the Quran and clarified by the authentic Sunnah. Some are stated directly in the Book of Allah; others are established through the Sunnah that explains it, since the Prophet ﷺ was sent to make the revelation clear.

Marriage as a covenant, not a casual bond

The starting point matters. Because the Quran calls nikah a mithaqan ghaliza, the same phrase it uses for the covenant taken from the Prophets, a Muslim should approach it with seriousness and structure, not the drift of a "talking stage." The conditions that follow are not bureaucratic hurdles. They protect the woman, establish lineage, and give the union a public, accountable standing. This is why the process is meant to be conducted openly and correctly from the first step. If you are beginning this properly, our guide on how to get married in Islam walks through the sequence in order.

Requirement 1: Two eligible, consenting Muslim spouses

Both parties must be lawful for one another to marry

A man may marry a chaste believing woman, and the Quran also permits marriage to chaste women of the People of the Book, while a Muslim woman marries only a Muslim man. Allah commands: "Do not marry idolatresses until they believe" (Qur'an 2:221). This is a condition of eligibility, not a preference.

Consent is required from both

No one, not even a father, can force a valid marriage. The Prophet ﷺ said, "A previously married woman shall not be married until she is consulted, and a virgin shall not be married until her permission is sought" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5136). When Khansa bint Khidam was married off by her father against her will, she came to the Prophet ﷺ and he annulled the marriage (Sahih al-Bukhari 5138). Coerced consent is not consent, and a nikah built on it is defective. The man's willing acceptance is equally required; both must genuinely agree.

Requirement 2: The wali (guardian)

A woman does not contract her own nikah. Her marriage is concluded by her wali, normally her father, then the nearest male relatives in order, and a judge or authority in their absence. The Quran addresses the guardians directly when it says, regarding women who have completed their waiting period, "do not prevent them from marrying their husbands when they agree between themselves in a lawful manner" (Qur'an 2:232). The very act of forbidding or permitting is placed on the guardian, which is why the Sunnah spells it out.

The Prophet ﷺ said, "There is no marriage without a guardian" (Sunan Abi Dawud 2085), and, "Any woman who marries without the permission of her guardian, her marriage is invalid, her marriage is invalid, her marriage is invalid" (Sunan Abi Dawud 2083), an authentic narration repeated three times for emphasis. This is a condition of validity upon the understanding of the Salaf, and it is not a cultural leftover to be argued away. If the wali is absent, unjust, or withholding without valid reason, the matter goes to the authority, not around the requirement. We cover this in more depth in our guide to the role of the wali in nikah.

Requirement 3: The mahr (dower)

The mahr is the woman's right, a gift from the husband to the wife that belongs to her alone. Allah commands: "And give the women their dowries graciously" (Qur'an 4:4). It is not a bride price paid to her family, and it is not optional.

There is no fixed minimum that makes it burdensome. The Prophet ﷺ told a man who had nothing, "Look for something, even if it is a ring of iron" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5087), and in the same incident married him to the woman in exchange for teaching her the portions of the Quran he knew. The lesson is that the mahr must be real and agreed, but it should be kept modest and easy, not inflated into a barrier. For how to set and document it, see our guide on the mahr.

Requirement 4: Public witnessing and announcement

Nikah is meant to be known, the opposite of a secret affair. The Prophet ﷺ said, "Announce this marriage" (Sunan Ibn Majah 1895), commanding that it be made public. The distinction between lawful marriage and forbidden relations is precisely this openness and witnessing.

Most scholars hold that two upright Muslim witnesses are a condition of the contract itself, based on narrations to that effect and the consistent practice of the Companions. A minority held that a sufficiently public announcement stands in for formal witnesses. What is not disputed is that a deliberately concealed, unwitnessed union contradicts the Sunnah and is at best gravely defective. The safe and correct course is to have the wali, two just witnesses, and a clear announcement. Where you find scholarly discussion on the exact form of witnessing, defer to a qualified scholar rather than pick the loosest option.

Requirement 5: Freedom from impediments

Even when everything above is present, the marriage is invalid if a prohibiting factor exists. The Quran lists the permanently forbidden categories in detail: mothers, daughters, sisters, paternal and maternal aunts, nieces, foster relations through breastfeeding, and specified in-laws, among others (Qur'an 4:23). Marrying within these degrees is void, not merely sinful.

Other impediments include:

  • A woman still within her iddah (waiting period) from a previous marriage or its ending. Allah forbids finalising a marriage until the prescribed term is completed (Qur'an 2:235).
  • A woman already married to another man.
  • Difference of religion in the impermissible direction, as covered above (Qur'an 2:221).
  • A man attempting a fifth wife while lawfully married to four, since the Quran caps plural marriage at four (Qur'an 4:3).

The wali and witnesses exist partly to screen for exactly these issues, which is why the process cannot be rushed or hidden.

The offer and acceptance (ijab and qabul)

The contract is concluded by a clear proposal and a clear acceptance in the same sitting: the wali offers the woman in marriage, and the man accepts, in the presence of the witnesses, with the mahr named. There is no fixed liturgical formula required, and no priest or clergy class in Islam to "perform" it. What is required is the meaning being unambiguous and the pillars being present. Anything beyond this that people treat as obligatory, elaborate ceremonies, specific chants, or cultural rituals presented as religion, should not be mistaken for a requirement of the nikah. For the practical drafting side, including conditions a spouse may stipulate, see the nikah contract.

What the Quran does not require

It helps to clear away what people wrongly treat as conditions. A lavish walima is a Sunnah to hold a wedding feast, not a condition of validity. A large mahr is discouraged, not demanded. Registration with a government office is a worldly protection worth having, but it is not what makes the nikah valid in the sight of Allah; the Shariah pillars do that. And a long period of unsupervised "getting to know each other" is not part of the deen at all. The lawful path to knowing a prospective spouse runs through the wali, honest inquiry, and a chaperoned meeting, not private courtship. Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Quran explicitly list every nikah requirement?

Not as a single checklist. The Quran gives the framework and several conditions directly, such as the mahr (Qur'an 4:4), the forbidden categories (Qur'an 4:23), and the guardian's role (Qur'an 2:232). The authentic Sunnah then details and confirms the rest, including the wali as a condition and the requirement of witnessing. The two sources are read together; that is how the Companions understood it.

Is a nikah valid without a wali?

Upon the strongest evidence and the understanding of the Salaf, no. The Prophet ﷺ said, "There is no marriage without a guardian" (Sunan Abi Dawud 2085) and declared a marriage contracted without the wali's permission invalid (Sunan Abi Dawud 2083). If a guardian refuses without a valid reason, the case is taken to the Islamic authority, who may act as wali. The requirement is not waived; it is transferred.

Is there a minimum mahr in the Quran?

The Quran commands giving the dower but sets no fixed minimum. The Sunnah shows it can be very small, even a ring of iron (Sahih al-Bukhari 5087). It must be something of real value that the woman accepts, and keeping it easy is closer to the Sunnah than inflating it.

Are witnesses actually required?

The marriage must be public, not secret; the Prophet ﷺ ordered that it be announced (Sunan Ibn Majah 1895). Most scholars require two upright witnesses as a condition of the contract. The safest and soundest practice is to have the wali, two just Muslim witnesses, and an open announcement. Do not rely on a concealed arrangement.

Can a Muslim woman marry a non-Muslim man?

No. A Muslim woman marries only a Muslim man. The permission in the Quran to marry chaste women of the People of the Book applies to Muslim men, while marriage to idolaters is forbidden outright (Qur'an 2:221). This is settled and not open to reinterpretation.

What if one of these requirements was missing after the wedding already happened?

This is a serious matter and depends on which condition was absent and the circumstances. Because rulings differ on how to correct a defective contract, do not self-diagnose. Bring the specifics to a qualified scholar who follows the evidence, so the situation can be assessed and, where possible, corrected properly.
