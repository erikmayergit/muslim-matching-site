The Muslim Population of the UK and What It Means for Marriage

﷽

In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Bestower of Mercy.

We try to follow and explain from the Qur'an and the authentic Hadith, upon the understanding of the three rightly-guided generations (the Salaf as-Salih). All good is from Allah alone, and any mistake or shortcoming is from ourselves.

TL;DR: The 2021 Census recorded around 3.9 million Muslims in England and Wales, about 6.5 percent of the population and the second-largest faith group. It is also the youngest large religious community in the country, which means a very large number of practising Muslims are at or approaching the age of marriage. That is the real story behind the numbers, and it is where a deen-first approach matters most.

What the census actually says

The Office for National Statistics recorded roughly 3.9 million Muslims in England and Wales in the 2021 Census, making up about 6.5 percent of the total population. That is up from 2.7 million (4.9 percent) in the 2011 Census. Islam remained the second most common religious affiliation after Christianity.

Scotland and Northern Ireland run their own counts, and their Muslim populations are smaller in both absolute and percentage terms. Taken together across the UK, the Muslim community is well over four million people. These are official figures, not estimates from advocacy groups, so they are a sound base to reason from.

A few things are worth stating plainly rather than dressing up. The community grew through both new births and migration. It is concentrated in particular cities and boroughs, especially in parts of London, the West Midlands, and the North of England. And it is diverse in origin, language, and school of thought, which shapes everything from family customs to how marriages are arranged.

The community is young, and that changes everything

The single most important fact for anyone thinking about marriage is age. Muslims are consistently the youngest large religious group in the census. A very high share are children, teenagers, and young adults, with a median age far below the national figure. The national median age sits in the low forties. For Muslims it is dramatically younger.

Think about what that means. A young population is a population moving into marriageable age in large numbers, year after year. There is no shortage of Muslims in the UK who want to marry. The difficulty is rarely the raw supply of people. It is finding someone whose intention, character, and practice of the deen you can actually build a home on.

Marriage is not an optional extra in this deen. Allah says, "And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy" (Qur'an 30:21). The Prophet, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, said, "Whoever is able to marry should marry" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5065). With such a young and growing community, the responsibility to make honourable marriage easy is on all of us.

Numbers do not solve the real problem

It is tempting to look at four million Muslims and assume marriage should be simple. It is not, and the reasons are worth naming.

Families are more spread out than they were a generation ago. The old model, where a marriage was arranged through relatives and known local families, has weakened as people move for study and work. Cultural barriers between communities of different ethnic backgrounds are still strong, even between practising Muslims who share the same creed. And the wider culture pushes dating, delay, and a consumer attitude toward people that leaves many exhausted and no closer to nikah.

So the number on the census page is not the same as opportunity. A large population that cannot meet in a lawful, serious way still produces frustrated men and women. The task is not to grow the number. It is to connect the people who are already here around the right criteria.

The criterion that should drive the search

The Prophet, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, was explicit about what to weigh. He said, "A woman is married for four things: her wealth, her lineage, her beauty, and her religion. So choose the one with religion, may your hands be rubbed with dust" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5090). The same standard applies to men. He instructed, "If there comes to you one whose religion and character please you, then marry to him" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1084, graded hasan).

Notice what the guidance does not do. It does not lead with looks or income. It leads with deen and character, and it treats everything else as secondary. This is the whole reason a serious platform should keep the focus on intention and practice rather than appearance. When you are choosing among a large community, the filter you use decides the quality of your marriage. If you create a Muslim Matching profile built around your deen and what you are actually looking for, you are applying the Prophetic criterion instead of the market's.

Marriage is done the Shariah way, not the cultural way

A large Muslim population also means a large amount of cultural baggage attached to marriage. Some of it is harmless custom. Some of it contradicts the Sunnah and needs to be dropped.

The wali is not optional

A woman does not marry herself off, and no cultural convenience overrides this. The Prophet, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, said, "There is no marriage without a guardian" (Sunan Abi Dawud 2085, authentic). He also said, "Any woman who marries without the permission of her guardian, her marriage is invalid, invalid, invalid" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1102, graded hasan). In a country where families are scattered and some sisters have no practising male relatives nearby, this needs to be arranged properly, through the father where present, or the correct guardian in his absence. It is not a formality to be skipped because it is inconvenient.

The mahr is her right

Allah commands, "And give the women their dowries as a free gift" (Qur'an 4:4). The mahr belongs to the wife, not her family, and it should not be inflated into a barrier that delays marriage for years. The Prophet, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, said the most blessed marriage is the one with the least burden (reported in Musnad Ahmad; treat the specific wording with care and verify with a scholar, but the principle of ease in marriage is firmly established in the Sunnah).

The contract should be understood

The nikah is a binding contract with rights and duties on both sides. Too many couples sign without understanding it. Read our guide to the nikah contract before you get to that stage so you know what you are agreeing to.

Culture is not the deen. Where a family custom clashes with the Book and the authentic Sunnah, the custom is what gives way. That is true whether the custom comes from South Asia, the Arab world, Africa, or anywhere else.

Getting to know someone without crossing lines

A young, large community living inside a dating culture faces constant pressure to do things the wrong way. The deen gives a clean alternative. You can and should learn about a prospective spouse seriously, but within limits: with the wali's knowledge, without seclusion (khalwah), and with the clear intention of marriage rather than open-ended "talking".

The Prophet, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, warned, "No man is alone with a woman except that the third of them is Shaytan" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2165, authentic). This is not a restriction designed to make life hard. It protects both people, protects the families, and keeps the process honest. A person who insists on secrecy and privacy before nikah is telling you something about their intentions.

This is exactly why a structured, purpose-built platform serves the community better than open social media or dating apps. When the goal is marriage from the first message, and the wali is in the picture, you avoid the drawn-out limbo that wastes years. What the data should push us to do

The headline is easy to remember: several million Muslims, a young community, growing, and spread across the country. The practical lesson is harder and more useful.

Marriage in this community is not blocked by a lack of people. It is slowed by scattered families, cultural gatekeeping, inflated expectations, and a wider culture that normalises everything except serious, guardian-involved, deen-first matching. Fix the process and the numbers take care of themselves. Allah promised, "And marry the unmarried among you and the righteous among your male and female slaves. If they should be poor, Allah will enrich them from His bounty" (Qur'an 24:32). The command is to facilitate marriage, not to delay it endlessly while chasing worldly criteria.

For the individual reader, the takeaway is simple. Sort your own intention first. Involve your wali or arrange the correct one. Weigh people by deen and character. Keep the process clean and quick. The size of the UK Muslim population is only an advantage if you use it the way the Shariah intends.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many Muslims are there in the UK?

The 2021 Census recorded around 3.9 million Muslims in England and Wales, about 6.5 percent of the population. Adding Scotland and Northern Ireland brings the UK total to well over four million. Islam is the second-largest religious affiliation after Christianity.

Is the UK Muslim population growing?

Yes. It rose from about 2.7 million in the 2011 Census to roughly 3.9 million in 2021 in England and Wales, through both births and migration. Because the community is very young, it will keep moving into marriageable age in large numbers for years to come.

Why is the Muslim community described as young?

Census data consistently shows Muslims as the youngest large faith group in the UK, with a median age well below the national figure and a high proportion of children and young adults. This is directly relevant to marriage, since so many are at or near the age of nikah.

Does a large Muslim population make marriage easier?

Not automatically. The number of Muslims is high, but families are scattered, cultural barriers persist, and the wider culture pushes dating over marriage. The bottleneck is a lawful, serious way to connect around deen and character, not the raw supply of people.

Can a Muslim woman marry without her wali?

No. The authentic Sunnah is clear that there is no valid marriage without a guardian, as in Sunan Abi Dawud 2085. If a father is absent or unsuitable, the correct guardian must be arranged. This is a requirement of the deen, not a cultural preference.

How should Muslims get to know a potential spouse in the UK?

Seriously, with the intention of marriage, with the wali informed, and without seclusion or secrecy. The Prophet, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, forbade a man being alone with a woman (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2165). A structured, deen-first platform is far safer than open dating apps or social media.
