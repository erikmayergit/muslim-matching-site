Nikah E Muta: What It Is and the Scholarly Verdict

﷽

In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Bestower of Mercy.

We try to follow and explain from the Qur'an and the authentic Hadith, upon the understanding of the three rightly-guided generations (the Salaf as-Salih). All good is from Allah alone, and any mistake or shortcoming is from ourselves.

TL;DR: Nikah e muta is a "temporary marriage" contracted for a fixed period in exchange for payment, after which it dissolves on its own. It was permitted briefly in the earliest years of Islam and then prohibited by the Prophet (sallallahu 'alayhi wa sallam) until the Day of Resurrection. Upon the authentic Sunnah and the understanding of the Salaf, it is forbidden. It is not a valid path to marriage, and no Muslim seeking a real, lasting nikah should go anywhere near it.

What nikah e muta actually is

Muta means "enjoyment" or "pleasure." Nikah e muta is a contract in which a man and a woman agree to a marriage for a stated, limited term. It could be a day, a week, or months. A sum of money is fixed, a duration is named, and when the time runs out the arrangement ends automatically with no divorce, no inheritance between the two, and none of the ongoing rights and duties that a real marriage carries.

On its surface it wears the clothing of marriage. In substance it is closer to a paid, time-limited encounter dressed up in religious language. That distinction matters, because Islam does recognise marriage as a serious, binding covenant, and Allah describes it as a means of tranquillity and mercy between spouses in Qur'an 30:21. Muta delivers none of that. It is built on an exit date from the very first moment.

Was it ever permitted? Yes, and then it was abolished

Honesty about the history is important, because the permissibility of muta at one point is not in dispute. In the early period, under specific and difficult conditions such as long military expeditions far from home, a concession existed. The companions themselves reference this.

What is decisive is that this concession was cancelled by the Prophet (sallallahu 'alayhi wa sallam) himself. 'Ali ibn Abi Talib (radiyallahu 'anhu) reported that the Messenger of Allah forbade the temporary marriage of women and the eating of domestic donkeys at the time of Khaybar. This is recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari 5115 and Sahih Muslim 1407.

Even clearer is the narration of Sabra al-Juhani (radiyallahu 'anhu), who was present when the Prophet (sallallahu 'alayhi wa sallam) stood and said that he had previously permitted muta, but that Allah had now forbidden it until the Day of Resurrection, and he ordered those who had such women to release them. This is in Sahih Muslim 1406. The wording "until the Day of Resurrection" leaves no room for it to be revived. A concession that was tied to a hardship was closed permanently.

So the pattern is the same as the gradual abolition of other things in the early community. A thing is tolerated for a season, then Allah removes it for good. Anyone who points to the early permission while ignoring the explicit, later, and final prohibition is telling half the story.

The position upon the Qur'an and the authentic Sunnah

The verdict of the overwhelming body of Sunni scholarship, upon the understanding of the Salaf as-Salih, is that nikah e muta is haram and the contract is invalid. This is not a fringe or hardline reading. It rests on explicit, authentic prohibitions from the Prophet (sallallahu 'alayhi wa sallam) that were transmitted by senior companions.

It is also supported by the way the Qur'an frames lawful intimacy. Allah restricts lawful relations to wives and describes anyone seeking beyond that as transgressing, in Qur'an 23:5-7. A muta partner is not a wife in the real sense the Shariah intends. There is no genuine intention of permanence, no mutual inheritance, and the "marriage" is programmed to self-destruct on a set date. It functions as a loophole, and the Lawgiver closed loopholes of this kind.

Because the prohibition is established by clear authentic texts, this is not one of those genuinely open questions where honest scholars still differ within Ahl al-Sunnah. The matter was settled by the Prophet's own words.

The verse people cite, and why it does not prove muta

The main scriptural argument advanced for muta is Qur'an 4:24, which contains the phrase often translated as "so for whatever you enjoy from them, give them their due compensation." Some read the word for "enjoy" (istimta') as a reference to temporary marriage specifically.

That reading does not hold up. The word for enjoyment in Arabic is general and applies naturally to the pleasure of ordinary, permanent marriage. The verse as a whole is speaking about lawful wives and the obligation to give them their mahr. Reading a whole institution of time-limited contracts into one general word, while ignoring the explicit prophetic prohibition that came afterwards, is not sound methodology. The authentic Sunnah interprets the Qur'an, and the Sunnah here is unambiguous that muta was abolished.

It is worth noting that a small number of early figures were reported to have held a permissive view at one stage, most famously Ibn 'Abbas (radiyallahu 'anhu). But it is also reported that he retracted or restricted that view once the clear prohibition reached him, and in any case the position of an individual companion does not override an explicit, authentic prohibition from the Prophet himself. The final ruling is the one that stands.

Where the Shia position differs

The main body that continues to permit and practise nikah e muta today is the Twelver Shia. Their jurisprudence treats it as a valid and even rewarded contract. This is one of the well-known points of division between Sunni Islam and Twelver Shiism, and it is not a small one.

From the standpoint of the Qur'an and the authentic Sunnah upon the understanding of the Salaf, the Shia position is rejected because it rests on rejecting or reinterpreting the sound narrations of prohibition that the companions themselves transmitted. When you have a clear report that the Prophet (sallallahu 'alayhi wa sallam) forbade the practice until the Day of Resurrection, and you have senior companions confirming and acting on that prohibition, no later contrary practice can restore what he closed.

We state this plainly, not to insult anyone, but because the reader deserves a clear answer rather than a fog of "scholars disagree." Within Ahl al-Sunnah the matter is not genuinely open. The disagreement is between the authentic Sunnah and a sect that departed from it on this issue.

How real nikah differs from muta

It helps to see what muta lacks, because the contrast shows why it was removed. A valid Islamic marriage is a serious covenant with the following features:

  • Intention of permanence. Spouses enter it seeking a lasting life together, not a countdown. A hidden intention to divorce can itself corrupt the marriage in the view of many scholars.
  • A wali. A woman's nikah is contracted through her guardian. This protection is a pillar for many scholars and never something to be waved away. If you are unsure how this works, our guide on the nikah contract walks through the essentials.
  • Witnesses and openness. Real marriage is announced and witnessed, not a private arrangement with a hidden expiry.
  • Mahr as a gift of honour, not a rental fee. See our explainer on understanding mahr.
  • Full rights and duties. Inheritance, maintenance, lineage of children, the waiting period, and a proper divorce process if it ends. Muta strips most of these out.

Once you lay them side by side, muta is exposed as a shell of marriage without the substance. The Prophet (sallallahu 'alayhi wa sallam) did not close it to make life harder. He closed it because it treated women and the marriage bond as disposable, and Islam came to honour both.

Why this matters if you are looking to marry

If you are a Muslim seriously seeking a spouse, the practical lesson is simple. Do not let anyone repackage a temporary, exit-dated arrangement as a "halal" shortcut, whatever label they attach to it. The same goes for vaguer modern versions of the same idea, where two people agree privately to "try things out" without a real, witnessed, wali-approved contract and without genuine intention of permanence. The form changes, the flaw is the same.

Seek the real thing. A proper nikah, entered with the right intention, through the right process, is where the tranquillity and mercy of Qur'an 30:21 actually live. Frequently Asked Questions

Is nikah e muta halal or haram?

It is haram according to the authentic Sunnah and the position of Ahl al-Sunnah upon the understanding of the Salaf. It was permitted briefly in the early period and then prohibited by the Prophet (sallallahu 'alayhi wa sallam) permanently, as in Sahih Muslim 1406 and Sahih al-Bukhari 5115.

If it was once allowed, can it be practised again?

No. The prohibition was stated to last "until the Day of Resurrection." A concession that was closed with those words cannot be reopened. The early permission does not survive the later, final ban.

Does the Qur'an not permit muta in 4:24?

The word for "enjoyment" in Qur'an 4:24 is general and applies to ordinary lawful marriage. It does not establish an institution of temporary contracts, and it cannot override the explicit authentic Sunnah that abolished muta.

Why do the Shia still practise it?

Twelver Shia jurisprudence continues to permit it because it does not accept the sound prohibition narrations that the companions transmitted. From the standpoint of the authentic Sunnah, that position is rejected on this issue.

What is the difference between muta and a normal nikah?

A normal nikah is intended to be permanent, involves a wali and witnesses, carries full rights such as inheritance and maintenance, and ends only through a proper divorce. Muta is time-limited, built to dissolve automatically, and strips out those rights. That is precisely why it was abolished.

Is a private "trial marriage" with an agreed end date the same problem?

Yes, in substance. Any arrangement entered with a hidden or agreed intention to end it after a fixed period carries the same defect as muta. Seek a real, open, wali-approved marriage instead.
