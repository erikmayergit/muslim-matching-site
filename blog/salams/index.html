Salams App: An Honest Look for Marriage-Minded Muslims

﷽

In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Bestower of Mercy.

We try to follow and explain from the Qur'an and the authentic Hadith, upon the understanding of the three rightly-guided generations (the Salaf as-Salih). All good is from Allah alone, and any mistake or shortcoming is from ourselves.

TL;DR: Salams (formerly Minder) is a large, mainstream Muslim dating and marriage app with a wide user base and video features, but its swipe-first design and light stance on guardianship and free-mixing make it a weak fit for a serious, deen-first search for a spouse. If your goal is nikah on the terms Allah laid down, you want a platform built around intention, character, and the walī from the first step.

What Salams actually is

Salams began life as Minder, a Muslim answer to the mainstream dating apps. It later rebranded to Salams, borrowing the warmth of the Islamic greeting. The name is well chosen. The Prophet (sallallahu alayhi wa sallam) tied the spreading of salam to love between believers, as reported in Sahih Muslim 54. A greeting of peace is a good thing. The question is whether the product around that name is built to bring you to a lawful marriage or simply to keep you engaged.

On the surface Salams offers what most people expect from a modern app: a large pool of profiles, filters for sect, practice level, and location, plus video calling and icebreaker prompts. It markets itself as marriage-focused rather than casual, which is a genuine step above the openly secular apps. That is worth acknowledging honestly.

Still, the core mechanic is the swipe. You are shown one candidate after another and asked to make a quick keep-or-discard judgement. That single design decision shapes everything downstream, and it pulls against the way a Muslim is taught to approach marriage.

Where the swipe model works against your deen

Marriage in Islam is not entertainment and it is not a numbers game to be optimised. Allah describes the purpose of a spouse in the clearest terms: "And among His signs is that He created for you mates from among yourselves, that you may find tranquillity in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy" (Qur'an 30:21). Tranquillity, affection, and mercy are the fruit of character and shared faith, not of a fast yes-or-no reflex.

The Prophet (sallallahu alayhi wa sallam) gave the criterion directly: "A woman is married for four things: her wealth, her lineage, her beauty, and her religion. So choose the one with religion, may your hands be rubbed with dust," reported in Sahih al-Bukhari 5090. Deen is the deciding factor. A swipe interface, by design, foregrounds surface impressions and trains the eye to judge on the very things this hadith tells you to look past. That is the deep problem with the format, and rebranding it with a peaceful name does not change the mechanic underneath.

There is a second cost. Endless swiping breeds a disposable mindset. When the next profile is always one flick away, patience and seriousness erode. People start collecting matches instead of pursuing a marriage. For someone who wants to be married and to guard their chastity, that is the opposite of helpful.

The walī question that most apps sidestep

Here is the issue serious sisters should weigh most carefully. A woman's nikah is not valid without her walī. The Prophet (sallallahu alayhi wa sallam) said, "There is no marriage without a guardian," an authentic narration recorded in Sunan Abi Dawud 2085 and Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1101. He also said, "Any woman who marries without the permission of her guardian, her marriage is invalid," repeated three times, in the same collections. This is not a cultural preference or an old formality. It is a condition of a valid marriage upon the understanding of the Salaf.

Most mainstream Muslim apps, Salams included, treat the guardian as an optional afterthought at best. The default flow is a private, one-to-one chat between two strangers, with the family arriving late, if at all. That structure quietly normalises the very thing the Shariah built the walī to prevent: a woman drawn into an emotional attachment with a man before any responsible party has vetted him.

A platform that respects your deen should make the walī part of the process, not a box you might tick later. If an app has no meaningful place for a guardian, it is asking you to run your marriage search on terms Allah did not sanction. Free-mixing, private chats, and fitnah

The honest concern with any open-chat app is what happens in the messages. Prolonged private conversation between a non-mahram man and woman, with no oversight and no clear intention to marry soon, is a doorway to fitnah. The Prophet (sallallahu alayhi wa sallam) warned, "No man is alone with a woman but that the Shaytan is the third of them," recorded in Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2165 and graded authentic. A private DM thread that runs for weeks is a form of that seclusion, even through a screen.

Allah commands the believers to guard their gaze and their chastity (Qur'an 24:30), and He tells us not even to approach the paths that lead to unlawfulness (Qur'an 17:32). A getting-to-know-you process is permitted and encouraged before nikah, but within limits: a clear intention to marry, purposeful questions about deen and character, and where possible the involvement of the family or a trusted third party. A design that hands two strangers an unlimited, unwatched chat channel makes it far too easy to slide past those limits. Speed and privacy are marketed as convenience. In practice they are where the risk lives.

What a deen-first alternative looks like

The fix is not to abandon technology. It is to use a platform whose defaults match the Sunnah instead of fighting it. That is the entire reason Muslim Matching exists, and where our approach differs from Salams.

  • Intention over impression. The starting point is what a person is like, what they practise, and what they want from marriage. There is no face-first swiping, because faces are not the criterion the Prophet (sallallahu alayhi wa sallam) told us to judge by. The wedge is character and deen.
  • The walī built in. For a sister, the guardian is part of the process by design, not an afterthought bolted on before the nikah. That honours the condition the Shariah made non-negotiable.
  • Purposeful, not endless. The aim is to reach a lawful marriage, not to maximise time in the app. A serious pool of people who actually want nikah beats a huge pool of people passing time.
  • Bounded conversation. Getting to know a prospective spouse should be focused and, where possible, overseen, keeping it within the limits Allah set rather than drifting into the open-ended private chatting that breeds attachment before commitment.

Allah encourages marriage plainly: "And marry the unmarried among you and the righteous among your male and female slaves. If they should be poor, Allah will enrich them from His bounty" (Qur'an 24:32). Marriage is half the deen and a means of protection. The tool you use to pursue it should push you toward that protection, not toward months of low-commitment browsing.

Is Salams haram to use?

Be careful here, because this is a matter of application, not a blanket verdict, and the ruling on any given use depends on your intention and conduct. An app is a tool. Using a marriage platform to find a spouse, with a sincere intention, involving your walī, keeping conversation clean and purposeful, and moving toward nikah, is a lawful use of a lawful means. Using the same app to chat idly with the opposite sex, to swipe for validation, or to carry on a private relationship with no serious intent, is not.

The problem with a swipe-and-DM design is that it makes the second path the easy one and the first path the effort. A platform whose defaults already sit inside the Shariah simply removes that friction. If you are uncertain about a specific situation, for example whether a particular arrangement counts as an impermissible seclusion, ask a qualified scholar rather than settling it yourself. Do not take a comparison article as a fatwa.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Salams the same as Minder?

Yes. Salams is the rebranded name of the app formerly known as Minder. The name changed and features were added, but the core swipe-based matching model carried over.

Does Salams involve the walī in a woman's marriage?

Not as a built-in requirement. Like most mainstream apps, its default flow is direct private messaging between two people, with family involvement left to the users. Since a valid nikah for a woman requires her guardian, as in the authentic narration "there is no marriage without a guardian" (Sunan Abi Dawud 2085), a serious sister should insist on involving her walī regardless of what the app defaults to.

Is using a Muslim dating app allowed in Islam?

Using a lawful means to find a lawful spouse is permitted when the intention is marriage, conversation stays clean and purposeful, seclusion is avoided, and the walī is involved. The design of some apps makes those conditions harder to keep. Choose a platform whose defaults help you obey the limits Allah set in Qur'an 24:30.

What should I look for in a marriage app as a practising Muslim?

Look for a platform that centres deen and character over appearance, that makes room for the guardian, that attracts people who genuinely want nikah, and that keeps the getting-to-know process bounded rather than open-ended. The criterion the Prophet (sallallahu alayhi wa sallam) gave was to choose the one with religion, in Sahih al-Bukhari 5090.

How is Muslim Matching different from Salams?

Muslim Matching is built around intention, character, and deen rather than swiping on surface impressions, and it treats the walī as part of the process rather than an afterthought. The goal is to reach a lawful marriage, not to keep you scrolling. You can join the Muslim Matching waitlist to start.

How do I keep the whole process halal from the start?

Make your intention marriage, involve your walī early if you are a sister, keep questions focused on deen, character, and compatibility, avoid private seclusion including prolonged one-to-one chatting, and move toward the nikah without dragging things out.
