The Nikah Contract (Nikah Nama), Explained

بِسْمِ ٱللَّٰهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

Bismillāh ir-Raḥmān ir-Raḥīm — in the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Bestower of Mercy.

We try to follow and explain from the Qur'an and the authentic Hadith, upon the understanding of the three rightly-guided generations (the Salaf as-Salih). All good is from Allah alone, and any mistake or shortcoming is from ourselves.

TL;DR: A Muslim marriage contract (nikah) is a binding agreement made valid by the consent of both spouses, the woman's walī (guardian), two trustworthy witnesses, a clear offer and acceptance, and an agreed mahr. It can include lawful written conditions, and any condition that contradicts the Shariah is void.

A lot of people imagine the nikah is the celebration, the food, the gathering. It is not. The celebration is custom. The contract is the religion. When you strip away the catering and the guests, what actually marries two people in Islam is a small set of conditions that the Prophet (peace be upon him) and his Companions established. Get those right and the marriage is valid, even if it happens in a living room with five people present. Get them wrong and no amount of celebration fixes it.

This guide explains what the Muslim marriage contract is, what makes it valid, what the mahr is and how it works, and what conditions you can and cannot write into it.

What the Nikah Contract Actually Is

The nikah is a contract ('aqd) before it is anything else. Allah calls it a solemn covenant in the Qur'an, using the words "a firm and binding pledge" (Qur'an 4:21). That framing matters. A wedding is an event. A nikah is a contract with rights and duties attached to both parties from the moment it is concluded.

The written document, often called the nikah nama in South Asian usage, is a record of that contract. The paper is not the marriage. The marriage is the agreement itself, fulfilling its conditions, with witnesses present. The document exists so the agreement is recorded, the mahr is written down, any conditions are preserved, and the rights of both spouses are protected later if there is a dispute. In most countries you will also want the civil registration so the marriage is recognised by the state, which protects the wife in particular.

So there are two layers. The Islamic contract, which is what makes the relationship lawful before Allah, and the legal paperwork, which protects you in this world. A serious couple secures both. For the bigger picture of how a marriage comes together from start to finish, see how to get married in Islam.

The Pillars That Make a Nikah Valid

Scholars list the essential elements that, if any is missing, mean there is no valid marriage. These are well established from the Qur'an and the authentic Sunnah.

The consent of both spouses

No woman can be forced into marriage, and neither can a man. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said that a previously married woman must not be married until she is consulted, and a virgin must not be married until her permission is sought (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). A woman whose father married her against her will was given the choice by the Prophet (peace be upon him) to keep or annul the marriage (an authentic narration in Sunan Abi Dawud and others). Consent is not a formality. It is a pillar.

The walī (guardian)

A woman does not contract her own marriage. Her walī does it on her behalf with her consent. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said, "There is no marriage except with a walī" (an authentic narration in Sunan Abi Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, and Ibn Majah). He also said that any woman who marries without the permission of her walī, her marriage is invalid, repeating it three times (an authentic narration in Sunan Abi Dawud and al-Tirmidhi).

The walī is normally the father, then the paternal grandfather, then the brother, then the closest male agnatic relative, and so on. If a woman has no suitable Muslim guardian, the authority among the Muslims (in practice a knowledgeable imam or Islamic centre) acts as her walī, because the Prophet (peace be upon him) said the ruler is the guardian of one who has no guardian (Sunan Abi Dawud, al-Tirmidhi). This requirement is not optional and it is not a cultural relic. It is part of the deen, and you can read more about it in the role of the walī in marriage.

Two trustworthy witnesses

The marriage must be witnessed. The reports on the wording vary in strength, but the established practice of the Companions and the position supported by the evidence is that the nikah is publicised and witnessed, not conducted in secret. Secret "marriages" with no witnesses and no walī are exactly what the Shariah closed the door on.

The offer and acceptance (ijāb and qabūl)

The contract is concluded by a clear offer from the walī and a clear acceptance from the husband, in the same sitting, understood by those present. There is no fixed magic formula required, but the meaning must be unambiguous: this woman is given in marriage, and this man accepts.

The mahr

A marriage without a mahr being owed to the wife is not how Islam structures the contract. We cover it in detail next.

The Mahr: The Wife's Right, Not a Price

The mahr is the payment the husband gives to the wife as a right that belongs to her alone. Allah commands, "And give the women their mahr graciously" (Qur'an 4:4). It is hers. Not her father's, not her family's, not a fund the husband can dip back into. The same verse says that if she willingly gives any of it back to him, then he may take it, which only underlines that it was fully hers to begin with.

A few points settle most of the questions people ask:

  • There is no fixed minimum or maximum. The Prophet (peace be upon him) accepted very modest mahr. In one authentic narration he married a man to a woman for a mahr of an iron ring, and in another for the man teaching her what he knew of the Qur'an (Sahih al-Bukhari). The point is that it must be something of real value, agreed and known.
  • Lighter is more blessed. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said that the marriage with the greatest blessing is the one with the least burden (an authentic narration in Musnad Ahmad). Inflated, competitive mahr demands that delay or block marriages run against the spirit of the Sunnah.
  • It can be immediate or deferred. Couples often split it: a portion paid at the contract (muqaddam) and a portion deferred (mu'akhkhar), owed to the wife and payable later or upon divorce or death. Whatever the arrangement, write it down clearly in the contract so there is no ambiguity later.
  • It is a debt until paid. A deferred mahr is a genuine debt the husband owes. It is not a token.

Keeping the mahr reasonable is one of the most practical ways a community makes marriage accessible. A father who loads the mahr to show status is not protecting his daughter. He is making her harder to marry.

Conditions You Can Write Into the Contract

This is where many couples underuse the contract. Islam allows you to stipulate lawful conditions, and those conditions are binding. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said, "The conditions most deserving to be fulfilled are those by which you make intercourse lawful," meaning the conditions attached to the marriage contract (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). 'Umar ibn al-Khattab held that a woman's rightful conditions in marriage are to be honoured.

So what kind of conditions are valid? Any condition that secures a genuine, lawful benefit for one of the spouses and does not contradict the Shariah. Common examples:

  • That the husband will not move her away from her city or country without her agreement.
  • That she may continue her studies or a lawful, modest line of work within Islamic limits.
  • That he will provide separate accommodation rather than housing her with extended family.
  • A clearly specified mahr and the timing of its payment.

A woman may also stipulate the right to initiate a divorce arrangement under certain circumstances. These matters can be sensitive and the exact wording carries weight, so it is worth setting them down carefully and, where needed, with a knowledgeable person's help. For how the contract connects to the rights that follow it, see rights and responsibilities in marriage.

If a valid condition is broken, the wronged spouse has recourse, which can include seeking annulment of the marriage through the proper channel. That is precisely why writing conditions down matters.

Conditions That Are Void

Not everything you can imagine is permissible to stipulate. Two categories are rejected.

First, any condition that contradicts a clear ruling of the Shariah. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said that any condition not found in the Book of Allah is invalid, even if it were a hundred conditions (Sahih al-Bukhari). So a woman cannot validly stipulate that the husband will never have the right to a second marriage in the sense of forbidding what Allah permitted, nor can a husband stipulate that he owes no maintenance, nor can anyone stipulate something Allah forbade.

Second, conditions that undermine the contract itself. The classic example is a fixed-term arrangement, a so-called temporary marriage (mut'ah), which the Prophet (peace be upon him) finally and permanently prohibited (Sahih Muslim). A nikah is entered into as a lasting bond, not a timed arrangement. Any "marriage" agreed on an expiry date is not a valid nikah at all.

Where a contract mixes a valid core with an invalid condition, scholars generally hold that the invalid condition is struck out while the marriage itself can remain valid, depending on the case. Because this gets technical, anything unusual should be checked with a qualified scholar rather than guessed at.

Practical Steps Before You Sign

A contract is only as good as the seriousness behind it. A few things to settle before the sitting:

  1. Agree the mahr openly between the parties, with the wife's genuine satisfaction, and record the exact amount and timing.
  2. Confirm the walī and that he consents and is present or properly represented.
  3. Secure two trustworthy witnesses.
  4. Write down any conditions in plain, specific language. Vague conditions cause disputes.
  5. Arrange civil registration so the marriage is legally recognised where you live.
  6. Make sure both spouses understand what they are agreeing to. Consent given without understanding is not real consent.

The whole point of doing the groundwork properly, including knowing a person's character and deen before the contract, is to enter the nikah on solid ground. That process should happen within the bounds of the Shariah, with the walī involved and without the private "talking stage" that the deen does not sanction. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a written document required for a valid Muslim marriage contract?

No. The nikah is valid when its pillars are met: consent, walī, two witnesses, offer and acceptance, and mahr. The written nikah nama is a record, not a pillar. That said, writing it down and registering it civilly is strongly advisable to protect both spouses, especially the wife, and to prove the marriage later.

Can a woman set conditions in the nikah contract?

Yes. The Prophet (peace be upon him) taught that the conditions most deserving to be honoured are those of the marriage contract (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). She can stipulate lawful conditions such as not being relocated against her will, as long as nothing contradicts the Shariah. Unlawful conditions, such as forbidding what Allah permitted, are void.

Who owns the mahr?

The wife, completely. The Qur'an instructs that women be given their mahr (Qur'an 4:4), and it is her property to keep or to give back willingly. Her family has no right to it, and the husband cannot reclaim it except if she freely returns part of it.

Is the marriage valid without a walī if both adults agree?

No, according to the position supported by the authentic Sunnah. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said there is no marriage without a walī, and that a woman who marries without her walī's permission has an invalid marriage (authentic narrations in Sunan Abi Dawud and al-Tirmidhi). If a woman has no suitable guardian, the Muslim authority acts as her walī.

What is the minimum mahr?

There is no fixed minimum in the strongest position. It must simply be something of genuine, agreed value. The Prophet (peace be upon him) accepted mahr as modest as an iron ring or teaching the Qur'an (Sahih al-Bukhari). Keeping it reasonable is closer to the Sunnah than inflating it.

Can a nikah have an expiry date?

No. A marriage agreed for a fixed term (mut'ah) was permanently prohibited by the Prophet (peace be upon him) (Sahih Muslim). The nikah is entered into as a lasting commitment. Any arrangement with a built-in end date is not a valid Islamic marriage.
