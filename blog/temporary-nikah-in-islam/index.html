Temporary Nikah in Islam: What It Is and Why It Is Forbidden

﷽

In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Bestower of Mercy.

We try to follow and explain from the Qur'an and the authentic Hadith, upon the understanding of the three rightly-guided generations (the Salaf as-Salih). All good is from Allah alone, and any mistake or shortcoming is from ourselves.

TL;DR: Temporary nikah, known as mut'ah, is a marriage contracted for a fixed period that ends automatically when the time expires. It is forbidden (haram) in Islam. The Prophet (peace be upon him) permitted it briefly in the earliest days and then prohibited it decisively until the Day of Resurrection. A valid marriage in Islam is entered with the intention of permanence, with a wali, witnesses, and mahr.

What "temporary nikah" actually means

Temporary nikah is the common English rendering of what the classical sources call nikah al-mut'ah, literally the marriage of enjoyment. In its technical sense it is a contract in which a man and a woman agree to be married for a stated period. When that period ends, the marriage dissolves on its own. There is no divorce pronounced, no waiting of the normal kind tied to a husband's decision, and often no intention of building a home, raising children, or sharing a life.

This is fundamentally different from the marriage Allah legislated. It treats the union as a rental of time rather than a covenant. And that difference is exactly why the scholars of the Sunnah rejected it.

Before going further, it helps to be precise. Two things are sometimes lumped together and should not be:

  • Mut'ah: a contract that openly states an end date. This is the temporary marriage under discussion, and it is prohibited.
  • A permanent nikah entered with a hidden intention to divorce later: a normal-looking contract with no stated term, but where one party secretly plans to end it. This is a separate problem discussed below.

The Islamic marriage is built on permanence

Allah describes marriage as a weighty, solemn bond. When speaking of the tie between spouses He calls it a mithaqan ghaliza, a solemn covenant, in Qur'an 4:21. A covenant of that weight is not something you set a timer on.

He also describes the purpose of marriage as tranquility, love, and mercy that settle between two people over time: "And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquility in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy" (Qur'an 30:21). Tranquility and mercy of that kind are the fruit of a life shared, not a fixed rental period.

The lawful outlet for desire in Islam is restricted to spouses and those the right hand possesses, as Allah says in Qur'an 23:5-6. Mut'ah dresses up what is really a temporary arrangement in the language of marriage, and the Sunnah closed that door.

The evidence that temporary nikah is forbidden

Mut'ah was permitted for a short time in the earliest period, during hardship on some journeys and expeditions, and then it was prohibited firmly. The prohibition is established in the most authentic collections.

'Ali ibn Abi Talib (may Allah be pleased with him) reported that the Messenger of Allah (peace be upon him) forbade the temporary marriage of women and the eating of domestic donkeys on the day of Khaybar. This is recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari 5115 and Sahih Muslim 1407.

Sabra al-Juhani (may Allah be pleased with him) reported that the Prophet (peace be upon him) had allowed mut'ah at one point, then said, "O people, I had permitted you the mut'ah of women. Indeed Allah has forbidden that until the Day of Resurrection, so whoever has any of these women with him should let her go." This is in Sahih Muslim 1406.

Two points follow directly from these narrations. First, the prohibition is not a matter of opinion or local custom; it is a clear statement from the Prophet (peace be upon him). Second, the wording "until the Day of Resurrection" removes any claim that the permission could return. A concession that was explicitly abolished until the end of time is not a live option for anyone today.

This is why the overwhelming body of Sunni scholarship across the generations, upon the understanding of the Salaf as-Salih, holds that temporary nikah is haram and that a contract stating a fixed term is invalid.

Why it was prohibited

You do not need the wisdom to accept the ruling, because the texts are decisive. But understanding it settles the heart.

A marriage entered with an end date built in undermines almost everything marriage is meant to protect. It treats a woman as something used for a season and released. It leaves children, if any are conceived, without the stable household the Shariah is so concerned to secure. It strips out the mutual obligations of maintenance, inheritance, and lifelong loyalty that Allah tied to the marriage bond. And it opens a wide door to using the form of nikah to reach what is really unlawful gratification.

Allah legislated marriage to build families and guard chastity within a lasting structure, as the rulings on the waiting period in Qur'an 65:1 show, since those rulings assume an ongoing relationship that is being carefully unwound, not a contract that simply evaporates on a date.

How real nikah differs from temporary nikah

A valid Islamic marriage has conditions that mut'ah either ignores or corrupts. If you are learning the process properly, our guide on how to get married in Islam walks through it in order. In brief:

Intention of permanence

A sound nikah is entered with the intention that it will last. Divorce exists as a lawful exit when a marriage genuinely breaks down, but it is described in the Sunnah as the most disliked of the permitted things. It is a remedy, not the plan. Mut'ah reverses this by making the ending the plan from the first day.

A wali for the woman

A woman's marriage must be contracted by her guardian. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said, "There is no marriage without a wali," reported in Sunan Abi Dawud 2085 and graded authentic. He also said, "Any woman who marries without the permission of her guardian, her marriage is invalid," recorded in Sunan Abi Dawud 2083 and graded authentic. Temporary arrangements characteristically bypass the wali entirely, which is one of many reasons they fall outside the Shariah. You can read more in our guide on the wali and why he matters.

Witnesses and public knowledge

Marriage in Islam is meant to be known and witnessed, not hidden. The Prophet (peace be upon him) instructed that marriage be announced. Secrecy is a hallmark of the unlawful, and temporary contracts are typically kept quiet.

Mahr as a gift, not a rental fee

The mahr in a real marriage is a gift and a right of the wife that honours her and marks the seriousness of the bond. In mut'ah the payment functions as compensation for a fixed period, which shifts its whole meaning. The nikah contract treats the mahr as part of a permanent covenant, not a price for access.

What about a permanent nikah with a secret plan to divorce?

This is a real question and it deserves a straight answer. A man contracts an ordinary marriage, states no end date, fulfils the outward conditions, but privately intends to divorce the woman after some time, for example while studying or working abroad. This is not mut'ah in form, because no term is stated.

Scholars have differed here. A number of them consider such a marriage valid in its outward form because the conditions were met and no term was uttered, while holding that the man is sinful and deceptive for concealing his intention. Others consider the hidden intention to make it resemble the forbidden mut'ah and rule against it. Because this is a genuinely contested matter, it is not something to settle from an article. Take it to a trustworthy scholar with the specifics. What is not disputed is that deceiving a woman and her family about your intentions is dishonest and a betrayal of the covenant Allah called solemn.

The related "misyar" arrangement, where a wife waives certain rights such as housing or a set share of the husband's nights, is likewise a subject of scholarly disagreement and is best referred to a qualified scholar rather than assumed to be either clearly permitted or clearly forbidden. What matters for our topic is that neither of these is mut'ah, and mut'ah itself is settled as forbidden.

The sectarian claim, addressed plainly

It is well known that mut'ah is permitted and even encouraged within Twelver Shia jurisprudence. This is one of the clear points where that school departs from what is established in the authentic Sunnah. The narrations quoted above, from 'Ali himself in Bukhari and Muslim, and the public prohibition until the Day of Resurrection reported from Sabra al-Juhani, are decisive for anyone who takes the Prophet's (peace be upon him) rulings as they were understood by his Companions and the generations after them.

So the disagreement is not a small difference of legal taste. It is a difference in whether the abrogation reported in the most authentic sources is accepted. Upon the way of the Salaf as-Salih, it is accepted, and temporary nikah is closed.

What to do instead

If the pull toward a temporary arrangement comes from loneliness, desire, or being far from family, the answer the Prophet (peace be upon him) gave is a real marriage, entered seriously. He said to the young men, "Whoever among you is able to marry, let him marry, for it restrains the gaze and guards the private parts," reported in Sahih al-Bukhari 5065. The instruction is to marry properly, not to invent a shortcut.

A serious marriage does not require wealth or perfect circumstances. It requires intention, a wali, witnesses, a mahr you can manage, and a partner chosen for deen and character. Frequently Asked Questions

Is temporary nikah halal in Islam?

No. Temporary nikah, or mut'ah, is haram in the view of Sunni scholarship built on the authentic Sunnah. It was permitted briefly and then prohibited by the Prophet (peace be upon him) until the Day of Resurrection, as in Sahih Muslim 1406 and Sahih al-Bukhari 5115.

Why did the Prophet allow mut'ah at first and then ban it?

Some rulings in the early period were relaxed under specific hardships and later finalised. Mut'ah was a temporary concession in difficult conditions that was then abolished decisively. The wording "until the Day of Resurrection" shows it will not return.

Is mut'ah the same as a normal nikah with a secret intention to divorce?

No, they are different. Mut'ah states a fixed term openly, which makes it invalid. A permanent contract with a hidden intention to divorce states no term, and scholars differ on it. In all cases, concealing your intention from a woman and her wali is dishonest and blameworthy. Ask a qualified scholar about specifics.

Does a woman need a wali for a valid marriage?

Yes. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said there is no marriage without a wali (Sunan Abi Dawud 2085, authentic). Temporary arrangements typically skip the guardian entirely, which is one more reason they fall outside a valid nikah.

Some communities practise temporary marriage. Does that make it allowed?

A cultural or sectarian practice does not change a ruling established by the Qur'an and authentic Sunnah. The narrations forbidding mut'ah are in the most reliable collections and are reported from the Companions themselves. Practice varies, but the evidence is clear.

What is the halal alternative for someone struggling with desire?

Marry properly. The Prophet (peace be upon him) directed young people to marry as the means of guarding chastity (Sahih al-Bukhari 5065). For those not yet able, fasting was recommended as a restraint. A real, permanent nikah, however modest, is the answer, not a temporary contract.
