The Nikah Ceremony, Step by Step

﷽

In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Bestower of Mercy.

We try to follow and explain from the Qur'an and the authentic Hadith, upon the understanding of the three rightly-guided generations (the Salaf as-Salih). All good is from Allah alone, and any mistake or shortcoming is from ourselves.

TL;DR: A valid nikah has a small number of non-negotiable pillars: a willing bride and groom, the bride's walī, two trustworthy Muslim witnesses, an agreed mahr, and a clear offer and acceptance (ījāb and qabūl). Around these the Sunnah adds the khutbah before the contract, du'ā for the couple, public announcement, and the walīmah afterward. Everything else is culture, not religion.

A nikah is not a performance. It is a binding contract before Allah that makes lawful what was unlawful, and it rests on a handful of clear conditions. Get those right and the marriage is valid, whether it happens in a masjid, a living room, or a modest hall. This guide walks through the ceremony in the order it actually unfolds, separating the pillars you cannot skip from the Sunnah acts that bring barakah and from the customs that people wrongly treat as obligations.

Before the Ceremony: Intention and Choosing Rightly

Everything begins with intention. Marriage in Islam is worship, half of the deen protected, and the standard for choosing a spouse is set before any ceremony is planned. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said a woman is married for four things, her wealth, her lineage, her beauty, and her religion, and he instructed: choose the one of religion, may your hands be dusted with soil, as reported in Sahih al-Bukhari 5090 and Sahih Muslim 1466. The same weight applies to a woman assessing a man: character and deen come first.

Allah frames the whole institution around tranquillity and mercy: "And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find rest in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy" (Qur'an 30:21). That is the goal the ceremony serves. If you are still at the stage of searching for someone upon the deen, do it within the bounds of the Shariah from the start. A platform built for that purpose, where intention and character lead, is what we designed at Muslim Matching, and the process of moving from search to marriage is covered in how to get married in Islam.

Step 1: The Khitbah (Proposal)

The khitbah is the formal proposal of marriage. It is not the contract itself and it does not make anything lawful between the two parties. They remain non-mahram until the nikah is concluded, so there is no dating, no private meetings, and no free-mixing during this period.

What is allowed and encouraged is a supervised, purposeful look and honest conversation to decide. The families are involved, the walī is informed, and both sides gather the information they need about deen, character, expectations, and circumstances. It is also forbidden to propose over another Muslim's accepted proposal, a boundary the Prophet (peace be upon him) drew clearly in the authentic narrations. Once both parties agree in principle, you move toward the contract.

Step 2: The Walī Gives the Woman in Marriage

The walī, the woman's guardian, is a pillar of the nikah, not a formality. Her father is the default walī, then her paternal grandfather, then her son, brother, paternal uncle, and so on among her male relatives in the recognised order. If none exist or all refuse without valid reason, the authority (a qualified Muslim judge or, in practice, a trusted community authority) acts as walī.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said there is no marriage without a walī, reported in Sunan Abi Dawud 2085 and Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1101, both authentic. He also said that any woman who marries without the permission of her walī, her marriage is invalid, repeating it three times, as in Sunan Abi Dawud 2083. This is a clear ruling from the Sunnah and it is not open to being quietly dropped to suit modern preferences. The walī protects the woman's interests; he does not force her, and a woman cannot be married against her will. Her consent is required, and for the previously married her clear verbal consent is needed. More on his exact role is in the role of the wali in marriage.

Step 3: Two Trustworthy Witnesses

The nikah is a public contract, the opposite of a secret affair. It is concluded in the presence of witnesses so that the marriage is known and the rights of both spouses and any future children are protected. The scholars require at least two trustworthy Muslim witnesses to the contract, and the Prophet (peace be upon him) commanded that marriage be announced rather than hidden.

Witnessing and announcement are two sides of the same principle. Concealing a nikah, treating it as a private arrangement between a man and woman, contradicts the whole point of the institution. If a genuine question arises for you about who qualifies as a witness in a particular situation, take it to a qualified scholar rather than guessing.

Step 4: Agreeing the Mahr

The mahr is the gift the groom gives the bride, and it is her exclusive right. It is not paid to her father and it is not a bride price paid to buy her. Allah commands: "And give the women their dowries graciously" (Qur'an 4:4).

The amount is whatever the two parties agree; there is no fixed minimum in the strongest view, and it may be paid immediately, deferred, or split. What the Sunnah discourages is extravagance that makes marriage a burden. The Prophet (peace be upon him) accepted very modest mahr, in one narration teaching a man to give even an iron ring, and in another marrying a man to a woman for what he knew of the Qur'an, as recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari 5087. Keep it meaningful and keep it easy. Inflated cultural demands that delay marriage for years run against the spirit of the deen.

Step 5: The Khutbah Before the Contract

Just before the contract is concluded, it is Sunnah for the one officiating, or the walī, or any capable person, to deliver a short sermon known as Khutbat al-Hājah. It opens with praise of Allah and seeking His help and forgiveness, the testimony of faith, and then the recitation of a few well-known verses on taqwā, including "O you who have believed, fear Allah as He should be feared" (Qur'an 3:102) and the opening of Surah an-Nisa (Qur'an 4:1).

This khutbah is a recommended act, not a pillar. A nikah is valid without it, but including it follows the guidance the Prophet (peace be upon him) taught his companions to open matters of importance. It orients the gathering toward taqwā before the commitment is made.

Step 6: The Ijab and Qabul (Offer and Acceptance)

This is the moment the marriage actually happens. The walī offers the woman in marriage, and the groom accepts, both in clear, unambiguous words, in the presence of the witnesses. A simple form is the walī saying, "I marry you my daughter [name] for the agreed mahr of [amount]," and the groom immediately responding, "I accept this marriage."

The key conditions here are that the offer and acceptance are connected in the same sitting, that they use plain language of marriage, and that both parties genuinely consent. Once the groom accepts, the contract is concluded and the two are husband and wife. Nothing more is required for validity. What follows is Sunnah and celebration. If you are writing or reviewing the terms, the nikah contract explains what may be included, such as valid conditions the wife may stipulate.

Step 7: Du'a for the Couple

After the contract, the Sunnah is to congratulate the couple with a specific supplication. The Prophet (peace be upon him) taught that when a person marries, they should be greeted with: "Bārakallāhu laka, wa bāraka ‘alayka, wa jama‘a baynakumā fī khayr" (May Allah bless you, and send blessings upon you, and join you together in goodness), reported in Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1091 and Sunan Abi Dawud 2130 as authentic.

Notice what is not part of this: there is no innovated group ritual, no fixed collective chant, and no cultural rite dressed up as religion. Sincere du'a for barakah, offered from the heart or in the taught words, is the Sunnah.

Step 8: The Walimah (Wedding Feast)

The walīmah is the marriage banquet, and hosting it is an emphasised Sunnah on the groom. The Prophet (peace be upon him) told ‘Abd ar-Rahman ibn ‘Awf to hold a walīmah even with a single sheep, as in Sahih al-Bukhari 5167. It publicises the marriage and shares joy with the community.

Accepting a walīmah invitation is also strongly encouraged. But the feast must stay within the Shariah: no free-mixing designed as a spectacle, no music and behaviour that displease Allah, no extravagance and debt to impress people. A blessed walīmah is a generous one within one's means, not a ruinous one. Keep the men's and women's arrangements separate and dignified, and the barakah of the whole marriage is better protected.

What Is Not Part of the Nikah

Many people treat inherited customs as if skipping them invalidates the marriage. They do not. Elaborate multi-day functions, specific colours, engagement rings exchanged with ceremony, coordinated entrances, and countless regional rituals are culture. Some are harmless and permissible; others carry free-mixing, music, imitation of non-Muslim rites, or wastefulness that a Muslim should avoid.

The deen made marriage light. The pillars fit in a single sitting: consent, walī, witnesses, mahr, and the offer and acceptance. When a community adds so many expensive expectations that young people delay marriage for years, that is a departure from the ease Allah legislated, not an expression of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a nikah valid without a walī?

No. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said there is no marriage without a walī, in Sunan Abi Dawud 2085 and Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1101. A woman's guardian must give her in marriage, with her consent. This requirement stands and is not something to work around.

Does the nikah have to be in a masjid?

No. There is no requirement for the ceremony to be in a masjid. It can be concluded anywhere the pillars are met, with the walī, witnesses, mahr, and a clear offer and acceptance. A masjid is a good and blessed venue, but it is not a condition of validity.

What is the minimum mahr?

In the strongest view there is no fixed minimum; it is whatever the couple agree upon, and it belongs entirely to the wife (Qur'an 4:4). The Sunnah encourages keeping it modest and easy rather than inflated. Where families dispute the amount, consult a knowledgeable person.

Do we need to sign an official document?

Islamically, the contract is concluded by the spoken offer and acceptance before witnesses, not by paperwork. However, registering the marriage with the relevant authorities in your country is strongly advisable to protect rights, and a written record is wise. The document reflects the nikah; it does not replace its pillars.

Can the couple meet privately after the khitbah but before the nikah?

No. Until the offer and acceptance are concluded, they remain non-mahram. Any getting-to-know-each-other must be supervised and within the Shariah, without seclusion (khalwah) or free-mixing. The lawfulness of the relationship begins only with the completed nikah.

Is the khutbah before the nikah obligatory?

No, it is a recommended Sunnah, not a pillar. Khutbat al-Hājah is encouraged before the contract, but a nikah is valid without it. The pillars are consent, the walī, two witnesses, the mahr, and the offer and acceptance.
